## Course Material

The goal of this course is to introduce first-year engineering students to modern computational environments used to solve engineering problems. In the context of engineering applications, basic procedural programming concepts will be covered. Emphasis will be on programming style, debugging, top-down design, and modular code. In addition to gaining knowledge of basic programming/coding concepts, students will develop basic problem solving skills. Students will learn computational thinking by creating algorithms to solve engineering problems which are frequently posed using mathematical formulae, and will translate these into executable computer codes in two different languages. Programming problems have almost infinite solutions, so innovative thinking and creativity grounded in good engineering principles is a must. Additionally, students will learn how to communicate the results of the solutions in several visual formats including plots.

Coding is an inherently creative process, in that it is an iterative process in which code is constantly modified due to feedback from the software and from instructors and other students. Specific topics are listed in the course schedule.

## Course Outcomes

As an outcome of completing this course, students will:

- Gain knowledge of basic procedural programming concepts
- Become proficient in the use of a modern computational tools
- Enhance their basic problem solving and debugging skills
- Develop experience in designing solutions to engineering problems using software
- Be able to document solutions to engineering problems

## BU Hub

This course covers Hub units in: Creativity/Innovation

### Hub Creativity/Innovation Learning Outcomes

1. Students will demonstrate understanding of creativity as a learnable, iterative process of imagining new possibilities that involves risk-taking, use of multiple strategies, and reconceiving in response to feedback, and will be able to identify individual and institutional factors that promote and inhibit creativity.

The entire class is about coding concepts. Coding is inherently creative. When writing code, one first comes up with an initial algorithm; this is then translated into code that is executed. Most code fails initially; it either has errors such as syntax errors (or "bugs"), or does not produce the desired results. The art of "debugging", finding and fixing the mistakes, is an integral part of the course. In many cases the algorithm must also be revised. So, coding is an iterative process that involves constantly modifying the code in response to a variety of feedback mechanisms. This is a learnable process. Students are shown common mistakes and how to avoid them, and they also learn techniques such as top-down design, and the use of modular code and function stubs in order to minimize mistakes. When solving problems in class and in the lab sections, students write and revise code until it "works", meaning that it does not produce errors but does produce the desired outcome(s). Students are then given feedback on the efficiency of their code from the course staff, and are frequently asked to revise their algorithm and code to make it more efficient.

Students work in teams to design their own problems and solutions that utilize the relevant programming concepts, and refine their solutions in response to feedback from the instructor, teaching assistants, and fellow students. Every day in class, students work in teams to solve problem sets designed to cover all of the important programming concepts covered that day. After each problem set is completed, the student teams compose their own problems, and solutions, that cover the same material and will write these on a discussion forum. The instructor and TA's check the solutions to all problems and suggest alternative solutions.

2. Students will be able to exercise their own potential for engaging in creative activity by conceiving and executing original work either alone or as part of a team.

Students work in teams to write their own problems and solutions every day in class. Additionally, students work in teams to design their own final project description and its solution, subject to constraints. The project must utilize a large data set and must solve a problem that will benefit society. Students must first write a project proposal; they get staff feedback on the proposal before continuing with the project. The project is done over the entirety of the course, and it builds on tools learned from problem sets that are given throughout the course. The coding for the final project is an iterative process in which students are given feedback at several points in the course.

## Course Format

The precursors to this course have been fully flipped for several years. This course will also be flipped, which enables the class section ("lecture") to utilize active learning. What that means is that instead of a traditional lecture followed by you trying to do homework, we flip or reverse that: you learn the material and answer assessment questions online before coming to class, and then class time is active learning; working on problem sets in small groups. All education research shows that students learn much more (and more deeply) from active learning environments and therefore we have spent hundreds of hours flipping the course, creating all of the supporting documentation that you will see online.

All students must be registered for a Lecture section, a Lab section, and a Discussion section. Note that the Fri 4:30-6:15 time listed under "Lecture" is actually the exam time; these dates are specified on the Syllabus.

The specific topics that will be covered in the classes are listed in the course schedule that follows. Students are expected to come to class fully prepared. This includes, as necessary, doing the reading, and viewing the slides and other documents in the Pre-class on BlackBoard. Students must complete all online assessment problems **before coming to class**.

The class period will consist of an active learning environment. During a majority of the morning class time, students will be actively working on problems first as individuals and then in groups. During the afternoon "lab", there will be a short lecture from the professor on a coding practice topic and then a homework assignment for the week to work on based on the material covered that day. These homework assignments may be problem- or project-based and are to be submitted to Gradescope by the end of the week.

It is expected that students will bring their own **laptops** to class and lab sections. Tablets will be insufficient to program on.

In the discussion sections on Fridays, the Senior TAs in charge of the section will review the week's material, and answer any questions that students may have. In most of the discussions, there will then be a short (20 minute) quiz on the material covered that week (the exact dates are listed in the syllabus). You may prepare a 5x7 index card with notes for the quiz.

## Grading

Letter grades are given only for the entire course, not for individual assignments or the exam. The cut-offs for the different letter grades will depend on the distribution of numerical grades at the end of the semester. Usually, the ranges are: 90 and above is an A (A- or A), 80 + is a B, 70+ is a C, 60+ is a D and below 60 is an F. (There may be a curve, but if so it would be in the favor of the students, e.g. an A- might go as far down as 89, but it would not be raised to 91.) **The cutoffs between the letter grades for a given range (e.g., B+, B, B-) will depend on the actual numerical grades and will not be determined until the end of the semester.** In order to pass the course, students must demonstrate a mastery of the material by having a passing average (>= 60%) on the quizzes and exams (combined weighted score, not all individual scores).

Numerical grades will be calculated for every student, based on the following percentages:

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Pre-class assessments | 10% |
| Class Attendance | 10% |
| Homework | 15% |
| Quizzes | 20% |
| Exam 1 | 15% |
| Exam 2 | 15% |
| Exam 3 | 15% |

Class and Lab grades include attendance. Some, but not all, grades will be posted on BlackBoard. In order to calculate your average during the course, you must keep track of all of your own grades. All grades become final one week after posting, except for the final project deliverable (which will be final two days after posting).

### Class Grade

Students are expected to be on time for every class, and are expected to be prepared for every class. Note that the reading covers all of the material, including the coding concepts. During the class period, students will be given sets of Practice Problems. Individual Practice Problems (IPP) are to be completed in the very beginning (first five minutes) of the class period, without using any reference materials.

These will be followed by Group Practice Problems (GPP) to be done in small groups; reference materials may be used for these problem sets. Students may NOT work alone on the GPP. Points will be deducted from the class grade if a student (a) arrives late; (b) is not prepared; (c) leaves early; or (d) does not answer Practice Problems. The class grade will be a combination of attendance, online, and in-class Practice Problem solutions, including the 5-minute IPP that will be graded. The IPP will be used for the attendance. If a student arrives late to class, after the IPPs have been collected, it is up to them to contact the TA's on arrival to be counted as present. **One class attendance grade and one IPP grade will be dropped.**

### Lab Attendance

For the lab, students will be given homework problems to work on during the lab period. Students will receive full credit for every lab for arriving on time and either completing all of the current homework problems, or spending the full amount of time diligently working on these problems. Points will be deducted from the lab attendance score if a student (a) arrives late; (b) is not prepared for lab; (c) leaves early without completing all of the homework problems (d) does not spend the time diligently working on the current problems; or (e) spends time working on other things (e.g. old ones not yet completed) or the project (or doing anything online) instead of the current homework. Missed labs must be made up as soon as possible. All homework problems must be completed (with all code blocks run before submission) and submitted on Gradescope. For the lab attendance grade, students must only work diligently on the problems during the lab, and may not use computers for any other purpose until the problem set has been completed. If the homework problems are not completed during the lab period, students must complete the problems on their own time.

### Homework

To receive credit for the week's homework, it must be submitted to the appropriate Gradescope assignment by 11:59 pm on Sunday. At this time the Gradescope assignment will close and submissions will no longer be accepted. A solution set will then be posted. Credit will be given to submissions made with a good-faith effort and will not be assessed for correctness. The solution set will represent one example of good programming style and a correct solution. There may be others. If you are curious about your particular solution, we encourage you to speak with course staff. **One missed homework grade will be dropped.**

## Generative AI

The instructors recognize that the use of AI tools provides new opportunities to accelerate the writing of programs including the development of novel software solutions to engineering problems. However, the primary goal of this course is for students to learn the fundamentals of programming and to gain a critical understanding of foundational concepts. We anticipate that later in the curriculum these foundational learnings, when paired with more advanced tools, will enable greater problem solving in many new domains. For this reason we discourage the use of AI tools as a shortcut to complete assignments. Instead, the course is designed to convey and practice concepts through hands-on application and repetition. This is how you will gain the desired experience required to be proficient at programming. In a nutshell:

- Doing assignments is essential to learn the material and develop programming literacy. Generative AI is good for scaffolds, but it does not replace human problem solving.
- The course is designed such that the assignments promote the learning of programming fundamentals
- The use of AI, plagiarism of other people's work, or inappropriate collaboration diminishes the learning experience
- Avoiding or shortcutting the assignments puts individual students **at risk of doing poorly on the quizzes and exams which are conducted off-line, with pencil and paper, and account for 65% of the final grade.**
